# A Decision Layer for Perishable Supply Chains
### What to buy, what to commit, what to promise, before supply is certain

*Prepared for Sarah AlSaleh, General Partner, Outliers Venture Capital*

---

**THE DECISION GAP**

Perishable operators commit supply to buyers before they know what their own suppliers will deliver. ERPs and inventory systems record what happened; they were not built to recommend what to commit next. That pre-commitment window has no standard decision layer, and McKinsey has found that most supply-chain planning still runs on spreadsheets. In perishables a wrong commitment resolves one of two ways, wasted product or a missed order, and the cost disappears into margin rather than onto a line anyone can manage.

---

**WHY PERISHABLES, AND WHY THIS MATTERS FOR THE GULF**

The product carries a clock, margins are thin, and the decision is irreversible once made. BCG's agrifood work identifies coordination failure, not only climate, as a primary driver of fresh-food supply-chain fragility. The Gulf feels this more sharply than most markets: the region imports the large majority of its fresh food, supply lines are long, and food security is a stated national priority in Saudi Arabia. Every imported pallet that spoils or order that goes short is a coordination failure paid for twice, once in margin and once in resilience. The operators who move that food have systems of record. They do not have decision support.

---

**WHAT WE DO**

We turn the messy CSVs an operator already exports into structured operational data, then run three things on top of it. A **diagnostic** that shows, in dollars, where margin is lost: waste, shortage, supplier fill and rejection, buyer fulfillment, and available-to-promise by product. A **compliance layer** that structures the lot-level traceability data regulators increasingly require (FSMA-204 in the US; the same structure serves Gulf import-traceability needs). And a **decision layer**, built on stochastic optimization over probabilistic supplier-yield and demand forecasts, that recommends how much to commit to each supplier and buyer and quantifies the overbuy and underbuy risk on each.

We prove it by backtesting: we replay an operator's own history and measure what the recommendations would have saved, so trust comes from their numbers, not our claims.
