# Where to sell, and where not to infer a market

## Recommendation

**MODIFY: offer a paid, read-only inventory and supplier-performance diagnostic
for an accessible mid-sized distributor; earn permission for a shadow planning
pilot.** Test Jordan first only if the founder has a warm decision-maker and
usable exports there. That is a distribution hypothesis, not evidence Jordan
has the largest market. Without that access advantage, run comparable outreach
to UAE import distributors and US regional specialty distributors and select
on paid diagnostic conversion plus data readiness—not country TAM rhetoric.

The initial operating target is one replenishment decision-maker, roughly
$20m–$100m annual sales, 1–3 stock locations and enough inventory/avoidable loss
to justify the fee. These are **screening assumptions**, not measured segment
boundaries. The actual qualifiers are inventory capital, controllable purchasing,
supplier history, documented rules and willingness to change a decision.

## US: large category does not imply an easy wedge

Regional foodservice, specialty-food importers, produce wholesalers and
independent multi-branch distributors are plausible discovery segments.
Do not generalize results from fresh produce to shelf-stable FMCG or to
service parts. The buyer is usually operations/purchasing, with the CFO
approving budget and IT approving data access. Ask who actually commits
inventory before discussing forecast accuracy.

The US already has documented foodservice and enterprise planning offerings,
and affordable inventory add-ons are a real alternative. A prospect may prefer
configuring its existing ERP or paying a known implementer. Security review,
clean exports, evidence of integration, accountable support and liability for
bad orders all create sales friction. Those are commercial hypotheses and
workflow requirements—not measured country-level CAC estimates.

Use Census wholesale tables to build an account universe, but distinguish
**firms from establishments**, eliminate retail-only entities, segment by
revenue/inventory, and count the buying group only once. NAICS 4244 sales are
not a software TAM. [2022 Economic Census wholesale tables](https://www.census.gov/data/tables/2022/econ/economic-census/naics-sector-42.html)

### MENA versus US

| Dimension | MENA investigation | US investigation | Decision implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Addressable count | Country-level qualified counts unknown | Better official business tables, still needs filtering | Do not publish false precision |
| Competition | Named GCC deployments; penetration unknown | Broad established offerings; prospect-specific installation unknown | Sell against actual configured baseline |
| Access | Founder/local-partner relationships may help | English outreach/ERP partners; no existing pipeline established | Warm data access beats abstract geography |
| Route problem | Route-specific import risks, not uniform MENA exposure | Importers also exposed; domestic distributors differ | Segment by controllable uncertainty |
| Fees | Country/account affordability unmeasured | Willingness to pay still unmeasured | Test identical paid-diagnostic offer |
| Implementation | Arabic/local templates and local support may matter | ERP/export diversity and security reviews matter | Record delivery hours and gross margin |

## Segment by economics, not just company revenue

| Annual sales band | Offer / rationale |
|---|---|
| <$10m | Usually diagnostic/template or channel-delivered offer; direct $25k subscription needs exceptional verified pain |
| $10m–$20m | Selective diagnostic; inventory and avoidable costs decide affordability |
| $20m–$50m | Initial candidate if buyer has authority and historical exports |
| $50m–$100m | Initial candidate; higher benefit potential, integration burden must stay bounded |
| $100m–$250m | Strong economics possible; multi-site governance/ERP requirements more likely |
| $250m–$500m | Partner-led departmental pilot, not ERP replacement |
| $500m–$1bn | Named workflow gap or region/category carve-out only |
| >$1bn | Enterprise procurement and established suites; avoid head-on platform contest |

For every band, collect SKU count, active suppliers, lead-time dispersion,
stock value, spoilage/expiry, emergency spend, planner hours, customer penalties,
available alternative sources, ERP modules used and decision frequency.
High sales with low inventory can be a worse prospect than lower sales with
costly, controllable stock. A broker that never owns stock is not the same ICP.

Recommended wording: **“We audit your current replenishment decisions and
identify changes worth testing—without replacing your ERP or automatically
placing orders.”** Add quantified savings only after the pilot measures them.

## Moat and build/copy/partner

Algorithms, a stock/service curve, public port feeds and a CSV uploader are
easy to copy. Integration quality, an auditable decision/outcome record,
consented supplier/route calibration, customer trust and repeatable delivery
may become harder to copy. No proprietary cross-customer network exists today.

| Asset | Now | Three-year defensibility path |
|---|---|---|
| Algorithms | Low moat | Keep tested/reproducible; do not rely on secrecy |
| Route model | Public inputs and assumptions | Consented actual lead/fill outcomes; benchmark incremental lead over operator knowledge |
| Distributor operating schema | Useful engineering | Stable mappings for packs, POs, receipts, credit, expiry and decisions |
| Customer outcomes | Missing | Versioned approved counterfactuals and real decision logs; no unauthorized pooling |
| ERP connectors | Integration work, not unique | Repeatable adapters and partner distribution; measure deployment hours |
| Replay and evidence gates | Useful differentiation | Trusted auditability and switching-cost record across conditions |
| Implementation speed | Unmeasured | Standardize first export to signed reconciliation and first usable recommendation |
| Relationships | Founder-dependent hypothesis | References, regional implementation partners, repeatable channel economics |

Year 1: three reconciled paid diagnostics and one approved operating pilot,
with full failure reporting. Year 2: demonstrate repeatability in one segment
and 1–2 ERP/export families before adding countries. Year 3: expand only where
retention, contribution margin and consented data advantage are established.
These are execution milestones, not revenue forecasts.

Build the reconciliation, common economics, decision evidence and buyer
workflow. Implement standard inventory methods openly rather than copying
proprietary software. Partner for ERP deployments, logistics execution,
specialized feeds and complex network planning. Stop building broad dashboards,
new models or autonomous purchasing until a specific paid problem requires them.
