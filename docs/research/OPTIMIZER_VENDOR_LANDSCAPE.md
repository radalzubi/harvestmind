*Desk research, 2026-09-04. Public sources only; vendor claims are labelled as such. No figure here is HarvestMind's.*

## 2. What the math actually is

Stripping the marketing, three families exist.

**Deterministic policy calibrated by statistics (the mainstream).** Forecast demand (exponential smoothing, ARIMA, now gradient-boosted trees such as LightGBM), estimate lead-time and demand variability, convert a service-level target into safety stock and reorder parameters, then run an MRP-style or MILP supply plan against those parameters. SAP IBP, Blue Yonder, Kinaxis, o9, RELEX, GAINS, ToolsGroup and Smart Software all sit here; Lokad's 2025 survey calls them "deterministic policies with stochastic cosmetics". Kinaxis's distinctive piece is an in-memory engine that recomputes the whole plan concurrently when anything changes; o9's is a knowledge graph plus Gurobi for the optimization; SAP IBP offers a time-series MILP optimizer or a finite heuristic; ToolsGroup and SAP IBP add multi-echelon inventory optimization (MEIO), which allocates safety stock across a network rather than per site. Independent reviewers note the suites disclose almost nothing about solver formulations or uncertainty semantics (Lokad scores o9 4.2/10 and Kinaxis 4/10 on that).

**Probabilistic decision systems (the minority).** Full distributions of demand and lead time, combined and pushed through to concrete order quantities ranked by expected economic return, with non-Gaussian tails. Lokad is the clear example (quantile forecasts 2012, quantile grids 2015, probabilistic 2016, "differentiable programming" 2019, all in its Envision DSL). Amazon's SCOT group is the other, at a different scale: model-based RL ("Direct Backprop" through a differentiable simulator) for periodic-review buying with stochastic lead times and lost sales, reported to beat newsvendor and model-free RL baselines in production, without published percentages.

**Heuristic / theory-of-constraints replenishment.** Dynamic buffer management (Onebeat, from the Goldratt school): simple rules that move buffers with consumption. No optimization, but robust and easy to adopt.

What none of the vendors publish: leakage-guarded closed-loop backtests against the customer's own prior policy. Forecast accuracy is the metric they sell on, and the M5 competition is a reminder of how thin that edge is: only 7.5% of teams beat the exponential-smoothing benchmark on accuracy, while uncertainty (quantile) forecasts beat ARIMA by up to 25%.

## 3. What they claim to save, and what independent evidence says

Vendor and consultant claims cluster tightly: 15–30% inventory reduction at equal or better service (ToolsGroup "20–30% less stock, 99% service"; Blue Yonder "5–15% inventory reduction, 10–20% fewer stock-outs"; McKinsey "up to 25% inventory cost reduction, 5–10 points of service"; e2open via Nucleus "5–35% lower operational cost"). Nucleus Research's audited RELEX study found about 20% inventory-cost reduction across four customers (22%, 17%, and two unquantified). Amazon and Lokad publish no percentages at all.

The evidence on realization is far weaker than the claims. Gartner reports that 76% of supply-chain technology transformations miss their success metrics, that even "successful" ones lose about 20% of projected value after launch, and that fewer than 30% of supply-chain AI pilots reach production. The peer-reviewed APS literature is case-study based, with authors noting the absence of statistical validation. Two-thirds of supply-chain managers still run planning in spreadsheets, including at companies that own SAP or Oracle. The famous failures were not math failures: Nike's $100M i2 loss (customization and dirty data), Hershey's $100M shortfall (big-bang go-live before peak season), Lidl's €500M SAP write-off (system logic mismatched the business).

Two readings follow. First, the 15–30% figures are measured against what the customer did before, which in most cases was a spreadsheet or a fixed cover rule, not a tuned policy; the same is true of HarvestMind's real-data result (+13% versus naive, under 2% versus a tuned incumbent). Second, the value delivered is mostly a function of adoption, data quality and change management, not of which solver is used, which is why the crowded enterprise segment competes on platform and services rather than on mathematics.

## 4. Where a small entrant could and could not fit

- The enterprise suite market is closed to a new entrant: 16 vendors, multi-million contracts, one-to-three-times-licence implementations, distribution through the big integrators.
- The mid-market is crowded on price and features (Netstock, Slimstock, Inventory Planner, GAINS at the top), but its products are still the "deterministic policy calibrated by statistics" family sold on forecast accuracy, and none of them shows a customer a leakage-free backtest against the customer's own policy or a guarantee of no harm.
- The one under-served combination is a regional one: FMCG distributors in Jordan and the Gulf sit on a single sea port, face documented lane disruptions, and are served by global suites through partners at enterprise prices, or by spreadsheets. Demand for *safe* automation plus lane risk signals there is plausible; its size is small (Saudi demand-planning software is a $142M market).
- What such an entrant must be able to show, and what this repository can and cannot yet show: (a) a real operator backtest against that operator's own policy (not done; P0 since the first pass); (b) no-harm behaviour under disruption (shown on synthetic data); (c) early warning from public lane data (shown on real history, five-week lead); (d) double-digit gains on the customer's weak SKUs (shown only in simulation and on the real-data-versus-naive comparison).
